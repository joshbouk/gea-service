# How a Blend report is written

This governs every word of generated client prose. It is not a preference.
A draft breaching the hard rules in section 6 does not reach a consultant.

The target is a report that reads as though a senior consultant wrote it on
a Sunday: specific, unhurried, willing to say a thing plainly and then stop.
The failure mode is prose that is fluent, balanced and says nothing. That
is what a language model produces when left alone.

---

## 1. The reader

A sponsor who paid for this, their leadership team, and possibly a board.
They are busy, numerate, and have heard a great deal of consulting language
before. They can tell when a sentence is doing work and when it is filling
space.

They did not ask for a thought-provoking read. They asked what is wrong and
what it costs them.

---

## 2. What a finding looks like

**State the finding. Give the number. Say what it means for them. Stop.**

> Sixty-four per cent of closed-lost opportunities carry no usable reason.
> The field is populated — "Other" is selected on 478 of 746 — so the
> dashboard shows a complete record. Nothing can be learned from it. Any
> conclusion about why deals are lost rests on the 268 that were filled in,
> which are disproportionately the deals a manager reviewed.

Four sentences. A number in the first, the specific mechanism in the second,
the consequence in the third, the limitation in the fourth. No preamble, no
summary line afterwards.

What this is not:

> It's worth noting that loss reason data presents a significant
> opportunity for improvement. While the field is technically populated,
> the predominance of "Other" selections — representing nearly two-thirds
> of closed-lost opportunities — suggests a deeper issue: the team isn't
> just failing to record why deals are lost, they're actively obscuring it.

Every clause in that is a tell. It opens with throat-clearing, hedges with
"suggests", uses an em-dash aside, builds to a colon reveal, and ends on a
"not just X, they're Y" flourish that makes an accusation the evidence does
not support.

---

## 3. Sentences

**Vary the length, but let most of them be short.** A paragraph of
uniformly medium sentences is the clearest signal of generated prose. Some
sentences should be four words.

**One idea per sentence.** Where two ideas are joined by a dash, a
semicolon or a colon, they are usually two sentences.

**Put the subject near the start.** "Marketing spend concentrated in events
accounts for 41% of the budget" rather than "Accounting for 41% of the
budget is marketing spend concentrated in events."

**Prefer the active voice, and name who did the thing.** "Sales leadership
reviews the forecast weekly" rather than "The forecast is reviewed weekly."
Where nobody owns it, that is the finding: say "nobody owns it."

**Never end a paragraph with a sentence that summarises the paragraph.**
The reader has just read it.

---

## 4. Words

**Numbers before adjectives.** Not "a substantial proportion" — "61%". Not
"a long sales cycle" — "a median of 94 days." Where there is no number,
there is usually no finding.

**Say the ordinary word.** Use rather than utilise. Help rather than
facilitate. Start rather than commence. About rather than approximately.
Because rather than due to the fact that.

**Banned outright**, because they appear in every machine-written document
and almost never in a good one:

delve, robust, leverage (as a verb), landscape (figurative), navigate
(figurative), unlock, harness, holistic, seamless, synergy, ecosystem,
journey (except a customer's actual journey), tapestry, testament,
underscore, pivotal, crucial, vital, myriad, plethora, realm, foster,
cultivate, embark, elevate, empower, streamline, optimise as a filler,
transformative, game-changing, best-in-class, world-class, cutting-edge,
state-of-the-art, mission-critical, paradigm, granular, actionable
insights, key takeaways, deep dive, double-click, north star, low-hanging
fruit, move the needle, boil the ocean, at the end of the day.

**Banned openers**: It's worth noting. It's important to understand.
Notably. Importantly. Interestingly. Crucially. That said. Having said
that. In today's landscape. In the modern era. As organisations
increasingly.

**Banned hedges where a number exists**: appears to, seems to, suggests
that, may indicate, could potentially, tends to. If the evidence supports
the claim, make it. If it does not, say what is missing instead.

---

## 5. Structure within a section

**No section preview.** Do not open a section by saying what the section
will cover. The heading did that.

**No section summary.** Do not close by restating what was said.

**Transitions are logical, not navigational.** "This matters because the
same field feeds the forecast" is a transition. "Now let us turn to
forecasting" is a signpost, and signposts are what a writer uses when the
ideas do not actually connect.

**Order by consequence, not by score.** The reader wants the expensive
thing first.

**One paragraph, one claim.** A paragraph making three claims is three
paragraphs or one claim with evidence.

---

## 6. Hard rules. A draft breaching these is refused

These are checkable and they are checked. Each refusal names the sentence.

1. **No three-part lists where two or four would do.** The tricolon is the
   single loudest tell. "Specific, measurable and owned" reads as rhythm
   rather than thought. Count the items, and if the third was added for
   cadence, cut it.

2. **No "not X, but Y" or "isn't just X — it's Y".** It manufactures
   profundity out of a definition. Say what the thing is.

3. **No em-dash asides.** An aside between dashes is almost always either
   the real point, in which case promote it to a sentence, or padding, in
   which case cut it. Em-dashes joining two independent clauses are the
   same problem.

4. **No colon-then-reveal.** "The pattern is clear: nobody owns it." Say
   "Nobody owns it."

5. **No rhetorical questions.** The reader is paying for answers.

6. **No scare quotes around an invented label.** If a thing needs quotation
   marks to be understood, name it plainly instead.

7. **No sentence beginning with a participial throat-clear.** "Looking at
   the data, we see that…" Just say what the data shows.

8. **No alliteration in a heading.** Three Ps in a row is a slogan.

9. **No client quotation over twenty-five words**, and no quotation at all
   without an attributable source.

10. **No figure that does not trace to a recorded metric or a client
    document.** Including a figure in a sentence as illustration.

11. **No recommendation in a findings section.** A sentence containing
    "should", "needs to", "must" or "we recommend" belongs in the roadmap.

Comparison to a peer group is governed by the release gate, not by this
guide. It is a methodology position that will change when Blend has enough
assessments to compare against, and a rule that moves does not belong
buried in a style document where nobody would think to look for it.

---

## 7. What to do with uncertainty

Say what is not known, specifically, in the same voice as everything else.

> The two sales teams record loss reasons differently, so the pattern
> below holds for the Regulated motion and may not hold for Retail. Twelve
> Retail opportunities carry a usable reason, which is too few to report a
> rate.

Not "further analysis would be beneficial". Not "this warrants deeper
investigation". Say what would settle it, and what it would take.

---

## 8. The two voices to avoid

**The enthusiast.** Everything is an opportunity, nothing is a problem,
every finding ends on an upbeat clause. A sponsor reading it learns
nothing and trusts nothing.

**The oracle.** Short declarative fragments. Portentous one-line
paragraphs. Claims with no evidence delivered as though evidence would be
beneath them. This is the register a language model falls into when asked
to sound confident, and it is the one that most reliably destroys a
consultant's credibility in a readout, because the first question exposes
it.

Neither is the voice. The voice is a person who looked carefully, found
specific things, and is telling you what they found.

---

## 9. The test before anything leaves

Read the first paragraph of any section aloud. If it could have been
written about a different company without changing a word, it is filler.
Cut it.
